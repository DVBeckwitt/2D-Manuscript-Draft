\section{Discussion and conclusion}

The ordered Bi$_2$Se$_3$ and Bi$_2$Te$_3$ test cases compare measured and calculated detector features after the calibrated geometry, weighted incident beam phase space, sample alignment, continuous mosaic distribution, ordered structure weighting, and finite detector integration are applied. The same coordinate map and finite profile operation are used for measured and calculated detector quantities. Within the fitted data set, the selected profiles agree in their principal peak positions and apparent line shapes, including features that would be difficult to interpret from a conventional one-dimensional reduction alone.

The low-$L$ Bi$_2$Se$_3$ region illustrates why the two-dimensional detector information must be retained. The long $003$ extension is radial in $2\theta$, while the mosaic-sensitive width is transverse in $\phi$. The full wavelength-weighted calculation reproduces the radial streak and the enhanced apparent $003$ signal. An otherwise identical mean-wavelength calculation collapses that extension without removing the transverse width. This separates wavelength dispersion from crystallite tilt and prevents the radial streak from being misidentified as evidence for a broad mosaic component. The $006$ feature occurs at approximately twice the radial coordinate of $003$, but its recorded intensity is not determined by the structure factor alone because the wavelength-resolved Ewald restriction and the low-$Q_z$ intensity envelope also enter the detector measure.

Keeping the comparison in detector space is important because the constraining intensity is distributed among off-specular arcs, cap-like $m=0$ features, radial wavelength-broadened streaks, transverse mosaic widths, and bright near-critical intensity. A conventional one-dimensional reduction can merge or remove these distinctions. Matched projection makes the data and calculation directly comparable, but it does not remove all correlations among mosaicity, divergence, alignment, and background. The off-condition $m=0$ features motivate testing a weak broad orientation component through their transverse extent. The displayed comparisons do not establish that this component is uniquely Lorentzian or necessary relative to a fairly re-optimized Gaussian-only model.

This distinction also defines the relationship to detector-space indexing software. Programs such as indexGIXS calculate and overlay allowed reflection positions and are the relevant prior art for the geometric indexing stage.\cite{SmilgiesLi2026IndexGIXS} The contribution here is the subsequent continuous detector-coordinate intensity and profile calculation. The same geometry is combined with the incident beam phase space, continuous orientation density, structure-factor weighting, wavelength-resolved Ewald restriction, and finite detector integration to compare measured and calculated feature locations and apparent profile shapes.

Standard planar-interface refraction and attenuation corrections are retained in the forward model. They are most important when an incident or exit angle approaches the critical-angle regime and are not presented as a new contribution. The near-critical $m=0$ response is therefore treated separately from the mosaic argument. Its low-$Q_z$ limit is described by Parratt reflectivity, while the higher-$Q_z$ Bragg response is described by the finite-stack kinematic calculation. The smooth connection between these limits is described as a Parratt-to-kinematic crossover. Figure~\ref{fig:specular_joint_fit_placeholder} reserves the joint comparison that will place the low-$Q_z$ and $003$ constraints on one detector-derived profile and one intensity scale.

The PbI$_2$ section shows how the same detector-space framework can be extended from ordered Bragg-profile comparisons to diffuse scattering from stacking disorder. In that extension, detector geometry, beam phase space, mosaicity, and the profile construction remain fixed, while the reciprocal-space intensity is generated from a stacking-correlation model rather than a perfectly periodic ordered stack. Diffuse intensity should therefore be interpreted as stacking information only after geometry and mosaicity have been constrained by the ordered-film workflow.

The staged workflow is part of the scientific argument. Instrumental parameters are determined first, sample alignment second, wavelength and mosaic-sensitive directions are separated third, and ordered or diffuse intensity parameters are introduced last. This ordering reduces parameter compensation and makes the final data and model comparison interpretable. The manuscript follows the same logic by showing the observation, identifying the physical direction of each effect, introducing only the model ingredient needed for that inference, and then comparing measured and calculated quantities on matched detector-derived axes.
