\subsection{Wave propagation and bandwidth-coupled refraction}
\label{sec:correlated_effects}
\label{sec:refraction}

The low-\(L\), \(m=0\) feature in Fig.~\ref{fig:hk0_low_l_star}
lies close to the direct beam and near the critical-angle region. In this
regime the measured intensity is not determined by an ideal external
Ewald-sphere intersection alone. Each ray is transmitted into the film,
refracted by the material optical constants, attenuated during propagation,
scattered from the mosaic population, and then refracted back into air before
it reaches the detector. The forward model therefore treats wavelength
bandwidth, refraction, transmission, absorption, divergence, and mosaicity as
coupled terms.

The key point is that the refractive index is itself evaluated across the
source bandwidth. For each sampled wavelength \(\lambda_j\), with spectral
weight \(w_j\),
\begin{equation}
  k_0(\lambda_j)=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_j},
  \qquad
  \tilde n(\lambda_j)
  =
  1-\delta_n(\lambda_j)+i\beta_n(\lambda_j).
  \label{eq:n_complex_lambda}
\end{equation}
Here \(\delta_n\) is the refractive decrement that shifts the phase velocity
inside the film, while \(\beta_n\) gives absorption. For electron density
\(\rho_e\), classical electron radius \(r_e\), and linear absorption
coefficient \(\mu(\lambda_j)\),
\begin{equation}
  \delta_n(\lambda_j)
  =
  \frac{r_e\lambda_j^2\rho_e}{2\pi},
  \qquad
  \beta_n(\lambda_j)
  =
  \frac{\mu(\lambda_j)\lambda_j}{4\pi}.
  \label{eq:delta_beta_lambda}
\end{equation}
Thus the finite bandwidth changes both the radius of the Ewald sphere and the
optical constants that set the internal propagation direction and attenuation.

For a uniformly scattering film of thickness \(t\), the complex normal
components give the depth-averaged propagation weight
\begin{equation}
  A(\lambda_j)
  =
  \frac{
  1-
  \exp\!\left[
  -2\left(
  \operatorname{Im}\{k_{t,z}(\lambda_j)\}
  +
  \operatorname{Im}\{k'_{t,z}(\lambda_j)\}
  \right)t
  \right]}
  {
  2\left(
  \operatorname{Im}\{k_{t,z}(\lambda_j)\}
  +
  \operatorname{Im}\{k'_{t,z}(\lambda_j)\}
  \right)t
  } .
  \label{eq:absorption_lambda}
\end{equation}
This propagation factor is multiplied by the entrance and exit transmission
factors, the structure-factor intensity, and the mosaic weight before the ray
is deposited on the detector.

The calculated image is therefore an incoherent sum over wavelength samples:
\begin{equation}
  I_{\mathrm{det}}(x,y)
  =
  \sum_j
  w_j\,
  I_{\mathrm{det}}
  \!\left[
  x,y;
  \lambda_j,
  \tilde n(\lambda_j)
  \right].
  \label{eq:detector_sum_lambda}
\end{equation}
The bandwidth therefore has two roles. It thickens the Ewald construction
through \(k_0(\lambda_j)\), and it changes the refracted wave through
\(\tilde n(\lambda_j)\). This is why the near-critical low-\(L\) feature is a
coupled optical and geometric effect, not a post-processing correction.

For the \(00L\) family, \(G_{00L}=2\pi L/c\). A fixed wavelength spread gives
an effective Ewald-shell thickness, and its angular overlap with the
mosaic-populated Bragg cap scales approximately as \(1/L\). Low-\(L\) Bragg
spheres therefore retain overlap with the mosaic cap over a broader range of
incident-angle detuning than high-\(L\) Bragg spheres. The long mosaic tail
supplies the tilted crystallites, while bandwidth-dependent refraction and
attenuation determine how much of that intensity reaches the detector.
