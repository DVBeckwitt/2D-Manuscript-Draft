\section{Introduction}
Layered van der Waals solids are often prepared as thin films, where their anisotropic crystal structures make crystallite orientation and phase central to the measured response.\cite{GeimGrigorieva2013,Jerng2013,Zhang2022Bi2Te3} Depending on the substrate and growth conditions, the crystallites may align their layer normals with the film normal while retaining little common in-plane azimuthal registry.\cite{Zhang2022Bi2Te3} We refer to this uniaxially textured limit as a two-dimensional (2D) powder: individual crystallites remain three-dimensional, but one crystallographic axis is preferentially aligned while their rotations about it are approximately random.\cite{Kaganer1999,Fischer2023MOFGIWAXS} The resulting reciprocal-space distribution produces characteristic specular and off-specular features that an area detector records simultaneously, providing access to their detector-space positions, apparent line shapes, azimuthal extents, and intensities for corrected quantitative comparison.\cite{SmilgiesBlasini2007,Hexemer2015,Savikhin2020}

The difficulty is that the same detector features can arise from several correlated experimental and sample-dependent causes, including finite wavelength bandwidth, beam divergence, grazing-incidence refraction, sample misalignment, mosaic spread, and disorder.\cite{Kaganer1999,Breiby2008,Savikhin2020,Smilgies2025Crystals} One solution is a forward model that starts from a crystallographic and structural hypothesis and propagates it through the incident beam distribution, sample geometry, scattering kinematics, and detector response to predict the area-detector image for comparison with experiment. Keeping the calculation in detector space allows the assumptions that generate each feature to be tested against the measured two-dimensional intensity distribution rather than only a one-dimensional reduction. Figure~\ref{fig:intro_2x3} places the 2D-powder state between the single-crystal and 3D-powder limits in real, reciprocal, and detector space.

Figure~\ref{fig:intro_2x3} summarizes the real-space, reciprocal-space, and detector-space consequences of these three orientational limits. In the Ewald construction, elastic scattering requires that reciprocal-space intensity lie on the Ewald sphere of radius $|\mathbf{k}|$.\cite{AlsNielsenMcMorrow2001} For a single crystal, reciprocal space consists of isolated Bragg points (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro_2x3}b), so at fixed incidence angle the Ewald sphere typically intersects only a small subset of reflections and the detector records sparse spots.\cite{AlsNielsenMcMorrow2001} For a 3D powder, random crystallite orientations smear the intensity of each Bragg point over a spherical shell, and the Ewald-sphere intersection produces Debye--Scherrer rings on the detector (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro_2x3}d,e).\cite{AlsNielsenMcMorrow2001} For a 2D powder, azimuthal disorder about the common \(c\) axis smears each off-specular Bragg point into an in-plane ring about \(Q_z\), while specular \((00L)\) reflections remain localized near the \(Q_z\) axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro_2x3}g).\cite{Kaganer1999,SmilgiesBlasini2007} The corresponding illustration in Fig.~\ref{fig:intro_2x3}f schematically shows 2D platelets with aligned growth, and a representative 2D-powder detector pattern is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:intro_2x3}h. When the Ewald sphere intersects this reciprocal-space distribution, the detector records hybrid signatures: typically paired spots when both off-specular intersections fall within detector acceptance, and single-crystal-like features near the specular stripe from \((00L)\) intensity.\cite{SmilgiesBlasini2007,Hexemer2015,Smilgies2005Rods} Mosaicity then redistributes intensity, broadening the ring intersections into arcs and spreading the \((00L)\) features into cap-like intensity near the specular region.\cite{Kaganer1999,SmilgiesBlasini2007}

This hybrid detector-space information is what makes the 2D-powder problem both rich and difficult. Many standard WAXS workflows reduce area-detector images to 1D profiles by azimuthal integration, often with masking near the direct beam and specular region.\cite{Ashiotis2015,Hexemer2015,Strzalka2018,Reus2024,Savikhin2020} Those steps suit nearly isotropic powders, but for 2D powders they deweight or remove off-specular arcs and specular caps that constrain out-of-plane correlations, stacking, and mosaic tails.\cite{SmilgiesBlasini2007,Hexemer2015} Stacking disorder is also commonly inferred from 1D interference models and refinements,\cite{HendricksTeller1942,Treacy1991,CasasCabanas2016} which do not directly exploit the two-dimensional anisotropy of oriented-film scattering. More general intermediate orientation distributions can be treated with orientation-distribution-function (ODF) refinements or preferred-orientation corrections,\cite{March1932,Dollase1986,MatthiesVinel1982,Pawlik1986,VonDreele1997,LutterottiVasinWenk2014} but those approaches are more general than needed for the narrowly constrained 2D-powder case considered here.\cite{VonDreele1997,LutterottiVasinWenk2014} Recent radial-profile approaches have made thin-film GIXD substantially more quantitative, but peak overlap and background separation can remain limiting in strongly anisotropic patterns, especially at small incidence angles.\cite{Gasser2025} For the layered films considered here, the measured morphology already motivates axial symmetry about the film normal and a low-dimensional out-of-plane mosaicity kernel.\cite{Kaganer1999,Smilgies2000POPOP,Fischer2023MOFGIWAXS} We therefore assume this physically motivated 2D-powder orientation distribution a priori and refine it with a detector-space intensity forward model, using off-specular arcs and specular-family line shapes as direct constraints.\cite{SmilgiesBlasini2007,Hexemer2015}


Here we present a detector-space intensity forward model for 2D-powder WAXS. Starting from an indexed crystallographic model, it predicts not only the detector positions of allowed Bragg features but also the area-detector intensity distribution after beam phase space, grazing-incidence propagation, mosaicity, detector resolution, optical attenuation/reflectivity, and structure-factor weighting are applied. The measured and calculated images are then subjected to the same projection and integration procedures, so the comparison tests local line shapes, relative Bragg intensities, and projected $Q_z$ profiles in addition to spot indexing. Prior work has established the main ingredients needed for such a framework, including grazing-incidence scattering formalisms,\cite{Sinha1988,Renaud2009} reciprocal-space mapping and indexing for oriented thin films,\cite{SmilgiesBlasini2007,Smilgies2005Rods,Hexemer2015,Savikhin2020} detector-space visualization and indexing of GIXS/GIWAXS patterns,\cite{SmilgiesLi2026IndexGIXS} and forward calculations of diffraction from textured films.\cite{Breiby2008} Most relevant here, Smilgies and Li showed that grazing-incidence scattering patterns can be indexed efficiently by calculating diffraction-spot positions directly in detector space rather than by nonlinearly transforming the full detector image into reciprocal space.\cite{SmilgiesLi2026IndexGIXS} The present work uses detector space for the calculation through detector geometry, grazing-incidence propagation, beam phase space, mosaicity, resolution, optical intensity terms, ordered structure-factor weighting, and disorder-dependent scattering to predict what intensity the detector should measure. Validation is centered on the ordered 2D-powder limit using Bi$_2$Se$_3$ and Bi$_2$Te$_3$ films.\cite{Jerng2013,Zhang2022Bi2Te3} After the ordered-film validation, we assemble PbI$_2$ as a diffuse-scattering extension in which stacking disorder changes the structure factor along fixed-$Q_R$ rods while the same detector-space projection and mosaicity framework are retained.
